# Achumawi historical-source supplement: inspection evidence

Reviewed 2026-10-19. Prepared for the parent researcher; no main data or Site was edited. The user-supplied study at `work/document-leads/achumawi.txt` was read as a lead list. It was not treated as an authority or as instructions. The six records and proposed paragraphs are in `work/achumawi-historical-05.json`.

## ac-history-1 — Merriam, 1926

**Actual content:** Smithsonian's 112-page scan, not merely its repository metadata. The title page gives December 31, 1926; text pagination runs 1–52 with additional plates and intervening scan pages. Closely inspected printed pp. 1–5, 12–14, 24–26 and selected entries pp. 41–47. Full readable content was obtained through the repository bitstream API:

<https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/cd9f1a23-00aa-4208-9a8d-49da591766c0/content>

The stable bibliographic reader link is:
<https://repository.si.edu/items/0625c939-2703-4eef-9eb9-b59714697028>

**Findings:** Introductory fieldwork and acknowledgments make named Indigenous contributions visible. The text distinguishes the narrower Fall River community from a wider grouping and records differences between speakers' names and outside labels. Its environmental discussion contrasts forested western and drier eastern country.

**Limits:** Early twentieth-century outsider classification, salvage assumptions, transliteration for English readers, and a historical map. These are not current constitutional categories, jurisdiction, or proof that living peoples vanished. The nine-versus-two framework is recorded historically rather than offered as a modern linguistic or political verdict. No precise village inventory or specialized ceremonial vocabulary is copied. The lead study's modern band spellings must be checked against current tribal sources rather than mechanically matched to Merriam's labels.

## ac-history-2 — CPUC cultural-resources chapter

**Actual content:** Read the complete relevant HTML subsections: ethnographic/archaeological background; historical background; McArthur Swamp history; cultural-resource surveys; regulatory setting and project effects.

<https://ia.cpuc.ca.gov/environment/info/esa/pgedivest/swaps/final/swapch_2v.html>

**Findings:** Useful project-area synthesis of violence and forced removal, return, wetland drainage, levees, ranching, and hydropower. It identifies incomplete survey coverage and tribal concerns, valuable qualifications to its own impact analysis. The broader packet includes a December 21, 2001 communication, so this is an early-2000s review; no exact report publication date has been asserted.

**Limits and rejected claims:** The report sometimes uses local and wider names loosely. Its Hokan/Palaihnihan wording is not accepted here as a settled present classification. Its regional archaeological dating does not date the beginning of Achumawi people or all occupation of their homeland. A list of fish includes introduced species; no blanket claim that every listed fish was a precontact staple is adopted. Its description of forced removal is not generalized to literally every person. Consultation is not consent. No archaeological site codes, burial locations, detailed sensitive maps, or current-law advice are reproduced.

## ac-history-3 — California State Parks brochure, revised 2016

**Actual content:** Four-page PDF, especially PDF pp. 2–3 on Ajumawi history, the fishery, springs, and native habitat. Copyright line says 2012, revised 2016.

<https://www.parks.ca.gov/pages/464/files/AhjumawiLavaSpringsWeb2016.pdf>

**Findings:** The brochure explains public-facing aspects of Ajumawi stone fish traps, spring-fed waters, spawning, harvest restraint, volcanic groundwater, and habitat damage. It identifies the Ajumawi as one of the eleven autonomous bands. Good local landscape interpretation, distinct from broader language or government information.

**Limits:** Agency interpretation, not a new community interview. The Fall River/Ajumawi focus cannot represent all Achumawi-speaking communities. Translation variants in the brochure are not treated as a universal linguistic ruling. The precise groundwater quantity and dated visitor regulations were omitted. Public maps were not used to reproduce sensitive cultural locations.

## ac-history-4 — Layton, 1981

**Actual content:** Eleven-page PDF, journal printed pp. 127–137. Closely read pp. 127–131 and checked the later reasoning pp. 134–135.

<https://escholarship.org/content/qt3793q75r/qt3793q75r_noSplash_bee433ed446bb01d884018abb42333e8.pdf>

**Findings:** A specialist synthesis of exchange networks, horses, captive-taking, and possible settlement effects. The introduction explicitly warns about fragmentary and uneven sources. Early pages distinguish relationships involving different Pit River communities; not every neighboring relationship is described as hostile.

**Critical limitation:** The author predicts defensive settlement changes but explicitly says protohistoric settlement studies had not been undertaken in Pit River territory. His Surprise Valley examples concern a different geographic and evidentiary case. The proposed prose preserves the distinction between hypothesis and demonstration. Victorian accounts and earlier ethnographies are not independent contemporary witnesses simply because the article cites them. Exact captive totals and monetary valuations were not adopted. A later artifact interpretation is acknowledged as speculation by the author and is not used.

## ac-history-5 — de Angulo / Boushey, 1975 translation of 1928 text

**Actual content:** All four PDF pages, journal pp. 60–63.

<https://escholarship.org/content/qt5b313642/qt5b313642_noSplash_425eb182ca392e0f043081d863756391.pdf>

**Findings:** Reported conversations about living relationships among people and their surroundings. The source itself identifies the older French publication and later translation. This supports a narrowly framed paragraph about historical representation.

**Limits:** The author's assertion of a complete creed, his speculative religious-development framework, stereotypes, and imagined reconstructions are not adopted as facts about all Achumawi people. This is not a current tribal statement or unrestricted permission to repeat specialized knowledge. The ritual greetings, offerings, and instructions printed in the essay are deliberately excluded. The collection includes this source only with a substantive critical-reading paragraph; remove it if that perspective does not fit the final profile, rather than retain it as padding.

## ac-history-6 — Pit River Tribe / CILS letter, December 10, 2001

**Actual content:** All four scanned PDF pages visually inspected after Poppler rendering, using the PDF skill. The signature identifies Michael P. Acosta as attorney for the Pit River Tribe. Local inspection files are `work/achumawi-letter-j.pdf` and `work/achumawi-letter-j-1.png` through `-4.png`.

<https://ia.cpuc.ca.gov/environment/info/esa/pgedivest/swaps/final/pdf/Letter_J.pdf>

**Findings:** Comment 2 (pp. 1–2) argues for considering transfer to the Tribe rather than the private recipient. Comments 3–4 (p. 2) challenge consultation and cultural-resource authority. Comment 6 (p. 2) addresses notification and tribal representation. Comment 7 (p. 3) corrects the proposed mitigation's political description of the Ajumawi band. The last page reiterates opposition and requests more environmental analysis. This distinct authored submission adds primary tribal advocacy, beyond the consultants' summary.

**Agency disagreement independently inspected:** Chapter 5, responses J-1 through J-13, was read:
<https://ia.cpuc.ca.gov/environment/info/esa/pgedivest/swaps/final/swapch_5.html>
The CPUC disputes several of the Tribe's contentions while accepting the band-name correction. Its responses should not be confused with the letter. The JSON cites the letter for the Tribe's arguments and makes no claim those arguments were adjudicated as correct, the transfer was prevented, or a present ownership outcome was established. Chapter 5 is background cross-checking, not an extra source added to inflate the six-resource count.

## Selection and integration

- All six actual works were inspected; each is substantively cited in a proposed paragraph. They are six different resources, not mirrored copies. The CPUC report and the letter have different authors, functions, and viewpoints.
- The generic CSUN handout, newspaper index, and bibliography leads were not included. They offer weaker or merely indirect support compared with the inspected full works.
- Current tribal naming, constitution, language scholarship, court opinions, and monument sources are outside this subtask and are being handled by the parent researcher.
- Readers need a coherent profile with contemporary community voices leading identity and government. These historical sources add evidence and questions; they do not replace living community authority.
- Remaining gaps include band-specific community history, public archaeological work directly testing regional hypotheses, current fishery stewardship, and the eventual outcome of the 2001 land-transfer proposal.
