## Sources inspected for this pass

The Lipan Apache Tribe’s visible “Who We Are” page was reread in full. The Tribe-hosted historical essays by Nancy McGown Minor on the Lipan homeland, relations with Mexico, the Republic of Texas, nineteenth-century military campaigns, and reservation-era movement were read in full. The Tribe’s 17th–21st century community-contributed timeline was read in full. These are distinct, topical pages on the same community website and are identified as a shared author/institutional perspective rather than independent corroboration. The 2023 Tribe-published cemetery project release was read in full. It identifies the Tribe’s collaboration with descendants, the Big Bend Conservation Alliance, and named Lipan Apache researchers; its plans are dated to the release and are not represented as completed current status.

Brandon Seale’s “Lipan Apache Indians” in the Handbook of Texas Online was read through its full body and bibliography. This is a substantial scholarly state-history synthesis. Its claims about political continuity, geographic scope, religious history, and present-day organizations remain attributed to the author; the books and archival references in its bibliography need separate review.

The Mescalero Apache Tribe’s “Our Culture” page was read for a short passage concerning Lipan arrivals and the Tribe’s constituent communities. It is retained as a supporting cross-community reference and excluded from the Lipan substantive count. The Fort Davis National Historic Site’s 2021 “Lipan Apache” page was also read in full; it is an existing substantive card. Its “no longer ... discernible presence” wording is attributed to the dated NPS page and is not used to imply that Lipan people disappeared. NPS gives 1936 for Lipan incorporation at Mescalero, while the Mescalero Tribe’s own page gives 1934; this remains unresolved.

## Primary-source and access limits

The Lipan Tribe’s landing pages for the 1838 Treaty of Live Oak Point and 1844 Tehuacana Creek treaty were inspected, but their linked scans were not readable through the available source-review tool. They are retained as leads, not inspected treaty texts. The page therefore describes treaty history through the community essays and TSHA synthesis and does not make independent claims about treaty wording, ratification, implementation, boundary, or Indigenous response.

The 2026 UTEP dissertation and 2016 UTRGV thesis repository records and abstracts were inspected. Automated PDF requests returned HTTP 403. That result does not establish a reader paywall; no controls were bypassed, and both remain excluded leads until substantive full-text inspection is possible. The introduced text of H.R. 10435 (September 16, 2026) was read on GovInfo. It proposes federal recognition and was referred to the House Committee on Natural Resources; it is an introduced bill, not enacted law, and is retained as a dated supporting record rather than a substantive historical work.

A Texas State Library and Archives catalog record, map 01413, surfaced naturally during the Texas source search. Its metadata describes a broad manuscript-map copy and lists Lipan Apache among its subjects. The image was not inspected, so no map-room item or profile association was added. The record’s catalog description and copying history are a future verification lead only.

## Synthesis and unresolved questions

The updated six-section reading path distinguishes the living Lipan Apache people and present-day Lipan Apache Tribe of Texas from historical bands, neighboring Apache communities, and the wider diaspora. It attributes the Tribe’s account of its unified contemporary community and multiple historical bands. It presents geography by period and does not convert source descriptions into fixed boundaries. It discusses relations with Spanish, Mexican, and Republic of Texas governments without treating linked treaty titles as verified treaty terms.

The Tribe-hosted reservation-era essay dates one Lipan group’s joining the Mescalero community to 1904; the Mescalero Tribe’s page says Lipan survivors arrived about 1903. These accounts could refer to related but distinct events, but this review cannot resolve that. The separate 1934/1936 incorporation dates likewise require further source-level review. The profile makes no current population claim and does not use the old NPS language to infer disappearance.

Next research should inspect the original treaty scans and related official records where legitimately accessible, examine the full Cabral and Leal theses if public reader access permits, and review work by Lipan Apache historians and researchers cited by TSHA.
