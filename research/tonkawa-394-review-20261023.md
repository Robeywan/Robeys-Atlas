# Tonkawa (original entry 394) — Texas research review, October 23, 2026

This review strengthens the Tonkawa directory source guide with seven substantive content checks and six supporting references. It remains an initial guide, not a completed deep dive or a community-reviewed history. The owner-supplied Jumano study remains separately catalogued; its URLs are leads, not Tonkawa evidence or inspected works.

## Current assessment

- Seven substantive works inspected; six supporting/context references excluded.
- Eight cited sections; assessment: **Still unfinished**.
- No present-day population, enrollment or acreage figures are inferred from undated pages.
- No historical map was inspected or added. No claim of full map assignment is made.
- The Tonkawa Tribe of Oklahoma is a contemporary community and government; older sources that speak of tribal disappearance are not used to negate that present existence.

## Substantive works reviewed

1. **Tonkawa Tribe of Oklahoma, “Tribal History.”** Read the complete visible page on October 23, 2026 (lines 17–46). It gives the Tribe’s self-name and its account of the 1884–1885 removal and commemoration. The page is undated and includes older stereotypes and generalized phrasing; these are not repeated. Its account is attributed to the Tribe. No linked map or photographs were inspected. [Source](https://tonkawatribe.com/language-culture/history/).
2. **Don Paterson, “Beginning to Learn Tonkawa: An Elementary Outline of Tonkawa Grammar.”** Prepared for the Tribe’s 2015 Language Restoration and Preservation Program series. Read the introduction, pronunciation guide, program/teaching rationale and first lesson material (PDF pp. 1–15 and 18–22). Associated audio and remaining pages were not checked. This is a language-learning publication, not a current program census. [Source](https://tonkawatribe.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Lesson1.pdf).
3. **TxDOT, *Tonkawa Tribe of Oklahoma Research Report* (August 2021).** Read PDF pp. 3–10 and 15–29, with selected bibliography entries pp. 29–30. It is a government chronology for planning and education. The project describes collaboration with participating Tribes, but this report does not identify Tonkawa review/approval; it is not characterized as Tribe-authored. Figures/maps were not visually inspected. Historical band relationships and casualty totals remain attributed to this report and its cited authors. [Report](https://ftp.txdot.gov/pub/txdot-info/env/toolkit/415-09-rpt%20.pdf).
4. **William K. Jones, “Notes on the History and Material Culture of the Tonkawa Indians” (Smithsonian Contributions to Anthropology 2(5), 1969).** Read the work’s introduction and historical sketch, selected conclusion/context passages and bibliography information. The paper places a historical sketch alongside a museum-object study based on objects collected at Fort Griffin in 1868. The complete object catalogue and maps were not inspected. Older colonial accounts, 1969 ethnological framing, and the proposed Toyah association are treated as dated or uncertain. No object details are reproduced. [Smithsonian/GovInfo PDF](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-SI3-PURL-gpo218631/pdf/GOVPUB-SI3-PURL-gpo218631.pdf).
5. **Treaty between Tonkawa head chiefs and the Republic of Texas, November 22, 1837.** Read the complete treaty transcription (printed pp. 28–29) in *Indian Papers of Texas and the Southwest*, volume I, a 1995 edited compilation. It records negotiated terms including reciprocal peace language and a Republic-appointed trading agent. It does not establish implementation or a complete Tonkawa political account. [Digital text, pp. 28–29](https://archive.texashistorytrust.org/view/49896224/34/).
6. **U.S. Congress, S. Ex. Doc. 52-13, 1892, transmitting the 1891 Tonkawa land agreement.** Read the transmittal, federal agency/commission reports, agreement articles and selected context (PDF pp. 1–6). This document records government representations, aggregate agreement terms and commissioners’ own reasoning. Individual allotment schedules and burial-location details are not reproduced or used. This is evidence of a federal position, not a neutral account of consent. [GovInfo record and PDF](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/SERIALSET-02892_00_00-012-0013-0000).
7. **Tonkawa Tribe, memorial concerning claims against the United States (adopted in council January 2, 1907; presented January 28, 1908), S. Doc. 60-206.** Read all six pages, including the council memorial, named member affidavits and appended federal-agent extracts. The memorial and affidavits challenge the 1891 cession terms and describe members’ later recollections and understanding. These are direct evidence of the Tribe’s claim, not a judicial finding or independent land valuation. The federal excerpts are separately attributed. Individual allotment and burial-site details are not reproduced. [GovInfo record and PDF](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/SERIALSET-05264_00_00-016-0206-0000).

## Supporting references not counted

- The Tribe’s **About / Tribal Profile** page was read in full. It supports the present government, location and constitution description; undated acreage, membership and population figures are omitted. The linked elder interview was not played. [Source](https://tonkawatribe.com/about/).
- The Tribe’s **Language** index and sound guide were read. A large linked dictionary PDF could not be read through the ordinary web reader; an ordinary request to the booklet returned 403. This is not recorded as a reader paywall, no access barrier was bypassed, and the dictionary is unread/un-counted. The separate Lesson 1 was inspected. [Source](https://tonkawatribe.com/language-culture/language/).
- The **Handbook of Texas Online** entry by Jeffrey D. Carlisle (1996) was read in full. It is retained only for comparing older historiography. Its 1951 “no longer distinguishable” statement conflicts with present-day Tonkawa governance and is not used as a statement of present identity. [Source](https://www.tshaonline.org/handbook/entries/tonkawa-indians).
- The **National Archives / Indigenous Digital Archive Treaty 246 catalogue** was read as metadata. It identifies the 1846 Council Springs treaty and Longwha (Tonkawa) as listed among multiple parties, but the treaty transcription itself was not read; no Tonkawa-specific interpretation is inferred. [Catalogue](https://digitreaties.org/treaties/treaty/175516187/).
- The Tribe’s **Powwows** page was read for scope. Because it mixes public event material with descriptions it itself identifies as secret, the profile does not reproduce those passages and the page is supporting-only. [Source](https://tonkawatribe.com/language-culture/powwows/).
- **Tonkawa: They All Stay Together** filmmaker pages were read, but the trailer, film, interviews and footage were not viewed. The production company describes collaboration and elder/leadership review; this claim is attributed to the filmmakers and remains unverified independently. The full film is a lead, not a content check. [Project page](https://www.tonkawathemovie.com/about.html).

## Gaps to resume

Prioritize further Tonkawa community-authored material; direct treaty/ratification and 1893 settlement-opening laws; primary Texas, Spanish/Mexican and federal records with their original context; accounts of the 1862 massacre from more than one perspective; and careful work on the historical band labels, language documentation and early geography. The 1846 treaty text, the 2020 Tonkawa museum booklet and the Tonkawa-English dictionary remain unread leads. More work is needed before calling this guide adequate. Maps remain paused unless ordinary research specifically supports a particular Tonkawa association and period.
