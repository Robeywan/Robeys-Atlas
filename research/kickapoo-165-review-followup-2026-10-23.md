# Kickapoo (Texas-focused) research review — October 23, 2026 follow-up

## Result

The existing Kickapoo people-level directory entry now has **12 substantive inspected works**, **6 supporting references excluded from that count**, and **6 cited profile sections**. The dated initial-adequacy judgment remains **Still unfinished**. This is a Texas-focused supplement about the Kickapoo Traditional Tribe of Texas and related cross-border history; it does not represent all Kickapoo communities. No historical map was added because this research did not inspect a specific archival map that warranted a new map-room/profile connection.

## New substantive works inspected

1. **Kickapoo Traditional Tribe of Texas, constitution packet.** The public, 20-page image-based scan hosted by the Tribe was read visually on pages 1–3 and 14–20. The inspected pages include the preamble and jurisdiction/governance text, the 1989 BIA approval certificate, and 2017 and 2018 election/amendment records. The preamble describes the Texas Tribe as organized separately and apart from the Kickapoo Tribe of Oklahoma. The scan also shows why the card avoids claiming it is the current consolidated constitution: its title refers to amendments A and B, while included election/approval papers use amendment labels and numerical designations that are not fully consistent. Individual names, rolls, and membership criteria were not transcribed. It is a primary government/legal record, not legal advice.

2. **Elisabeth A. Mager Hois, “Historia de la tribu kikapú y de su problemática” (2010), UNAM.** The full 13-page Spanish chapter (printed pages 101–113) was read. It surveys historical movement from the Great Lakes and Midwest through Texas and Coahuila, El Nacimiento, land, and the Texas community’s later political/economic history. The chapter draws on earlier published works and interviews with Kickapoo and Múzquiz residents reported for 1996–2002; its interpretations are attributed to the author. Its footnotes explicitly preserve disagreements among cited authors, including dates for the 1873 El Remolino attack and land-area totals. These are left unresolved. The repository lists the work as open access under CC BY-NC-ND 4.0; the atlas links to it without reproducing its text.

3. **José Luis Moctezuma Zamarrón and Fernando Nava López, “El sistema fonológico del Kickapoo de Coahuila…” (2011), INALI.** Selected substantial sections were read from the 146-page PDF: printed pages 13–23 (research scope, fieldwork, geography/history), 30–45 (ethnographic and language-use description), and 109–120 (practical orthography). The author reports six months of fieldwork from 1980–1983 and later visits in 1988 and 2010. The final chapter describes consultation and community decision-making in the practical writing system. This is a dated linguistic and ethnographic study of the Coahuila variety, not a current survey of language use or an account of every Kickapoo community. Restricted cultural descriptions were excluded. INALI’s publication carries an all-rights-reserved notice, so the atlas links to it without copying text or figures.

## Additional historical works inspected in this follow-up

4. **“No-ko-aht’s Talk” (Kansas Historical Quarterly, 1932), recording a 1867 account attributed to a Kickapoo leader.** The full transcription and notes (pp.153–159) were read. The text was taken down in shorthand by federal agent Franklin G. Adams and edited by George A. Root. It presents an attributed first-person account of a journey between Kansas and Mexico and the Dove Creek attack, but the editor’s preface and the account differ in dates and group counts. That mediation and discrepancy remain explicit; the source is not treated as verbatim or independently conclusive.

5. **U.S. Department of State, Foreign Relations of the United States, 1875, vol. II, document 444.** The full indexed document was read, including a translated diplomatic exchange and attached Coahuila reports. It documents competing U.S. and Mexican government positions over efforts to move Kickapoo and other Indigenous people from Mexico to U.S. reservations. The Coahuila reports say some Kickapoo people remained at Nacimiento and elsewhere; reported numbers and claims are attributed to officials. The removal framing, colonial language, translation, and missing community perspective prevent treating the file as a neutral or complete account of consent, movement, or residence.

6. **William C. Pool, “The Battle of Dove Creek” (1950), Southwestern Historical Quarterly 53(4).** Selected pages 367, 375, 379, and 385 were inspected through the UNT Portal. The excerpt frames the January 1865 battle through military records and describes the attackers’ failure to identify the camp or establish its strength. Only those four pages were inspected; the rest of the 19-page article remains unread, and automated OCR may contain errors. It is useful for a later military-history reconstruction, not a Kickapoo community account or independent confirmation of every casualty or movement claim.

7. **Elisabeth A. Mager, translated by Heather Dashner Monk, “Ethnic Consciousness in Cultural Survival: The Morongo Band of Mission Indians and the Kickapoo Traditional Tribe of Texas” (2017), *American Indian Culture and Research Journal* 41(1), 47–72.** Selected pages 47–48 and 61–63, plus the relevant notes on printed page 71, were inspected in the openly available scholarly PDF. The methods note describes documentary research, participant observation and interviews; the Kickapoo section discusses land acquisition and later economic/education history. The author’s cited interviews and fieldwork span 1995–2015, so these are dated research, not current conditions. Mager’s comparative argument is interpretation, and she is also the author of the 2010 and 2020 works in this collection; overlapping assertions are not independent corroboration. Other cultural descriptions outside the sections used here were not incorporated. The repository identifies a CC BY-NC license; the atlas links to the article without reproducing it.

## Supporting references and search decisions

- The INALI language-atlas page was read as a dated government reference and retained as supporting context only. Its risk classification and counts draw on 2000/2010 census-era information; those figures are not used as current population or language-status facts. Its interpretive statements about cultural change are not adopted as established findings.
- The brief KTTT education page remains supporting only: it describes departmental aims and a language program, while several initiatives are marked under development.
- The TAMIU 2025 thesis repository page and abstract were reviewed; a direct automated full-text request returned 403. That response does not establish a reader paywall. The full thesis remains unread and uncounted.
- The 2017 Texas Tribal Strategic Advisory Group / DPS report remains one substantive work already counted. Its schematic location/migration image is not treated as a homeland boundary.
- The 2024 article about language and psychological health exposes only a restricted abstract in the inspected publisher page, and the abstract does not identify the sampled Kickapoo community. It was not counted or used for the Texas-specific synthesis.
- The Milwaukee Public Museum history page was screened. It summarizes earlier literature (especially the Latorre 1976 book) and includes old population and band-count statements; it is not added as a substantive inspected work or relied on for current facts.
- The INAH page for a Kickapoo body-part study and the INAH article about the Rio Grande were surfaced, but normal web requests timed out. No content claims are drawn from those failed requests and neither is counted.
- The NILL constitution gateway is a catalog record, not a separate substantive work. The Tribe-hosted primary scan is the source used.
- The Benson Latin American Collection finding aid for the Latorre Collection on Kickapoo Indigenous peoples of Mexico was read in full as an archival description, not as collection contents. Its scope notes identify materials chiefly concerning El Nacimiento from 1960–1975, including field notes, manuscripts, photographs and correspondence. The finding aid states restrictions on cell-phone photography and that publication/exhibition permission is not granted pending further notice. It is retained as a supporting archival lead only; no collection item was accessed, and it does not count as a substantive inspected work.

## Coverage and limits

The expanded profile now connects the constitution packet to the legal chronology, the 2010 chapter to cross-border and historical sections, and the 2011 INALI study to the language/orthography section. Repeated claims across Mager Hois’s two inspected works are identified as works by the same author and are not treated as independent corroboration. The federal act and solicitor memorandum remain limited to their own legal questions and dates. HUD’s brief case feature remains a dated housing example, not evidence of present conditions.

Important gaps remain: more substantive Texas Tribe-authored history and education/language publications; current consolidated constitutional and administrative records; full review of the 2025 TAMIU thesis if accessible through ordinary public or library access; distinct viewpoints from Kickapoo communities in Kansas, Oklahoma, and Coahuila; reconciliation of conflicting migration and 1873 event accounts; current language-use evidence; and community review. Sparse or disputed evidence is not filled with speculative claims. No community review or endorsement is recorded. A careful search has not established that these selected works provide balanced representation across the distinct Kickapoo governments and communities; the twelve-source target is reached, but initial adequacy remains **Still unfinished**.
