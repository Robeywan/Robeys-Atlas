# Ysleta del Sur Pueblo — Texas research follow-up

Reviewed October 23, 2026. This follow-up extends the initial public research guide with the 1987 Senate committee report and a substantial 2021 TxDOT historical report developed in consultation with Ysleta del Sur Pueblo’s Department of Cultural Preservation. The report is cited for its stated method and for broad, attributed historical context. It is not characterized as a Pueblo-authored monograph or independent corroboration of every historical interpretation.

## New substantive work reviewed

**Texas Department of Transportation, *Tribal Histories: Ysleta del Sur Pueblo Research Report* (July 2021).** [Official PDF](https://ftp.txdot.gov/pub/txdot-info/env/toolkit/415-11-rpt.pdf). The report’s introduction (PDF p. 3) says the source materials were recommended and approved by the Pueblo’s Director of Cultural Preservation, who also commented on the draft. TxDOT explains that the report was prepared as a geographic historical narrative for transportation planning, public engagement and educational use. I read substantive pages 3–4, 21–22 and 28–35, plus the bibliography on 37–40. The reviewed sections discuss the report’s research method, Pueblo Revolt-era movement as represented in cited accounts, establishment and agriculture at Ysleta, land-tenure records, military service, later recognition and the history-writing process. They support a cautiously sourced account, while the underlying colonial records and secondary interpretations remain attributed to the report’s bibliography. The PDF was retrieved from the official TxDOT host as a public PDF; no subscription or access requirement was observed in this session. The PDF’s maps, photographs, appendices and GIS data were not independently inspected.

The report’s separate section on resources identified as culturally sensitive for future planning (PDF p. 36) was not used in the profile synthesis. No location-level data, coordinates or sensitive-site details were copied. Although the report’s consultation process is valuable evidence of provenance, this remains a government research product and should not be mistaken for direct Pueblo authorship or formal endorsement of every statement.

## Updated profile status

The guide now records eight substantive inspected works, two supporting references excluded from the substantive count, six cited sections, and **Adequate initial introduction** as a dated starting assessment. It remains unfinished and is not represented as complete or community-reviewed. The new TxDOT report strengthens coverage of movement, settlement, land history and the development of historical accounts. Remaining needs include additional Pueblo-authored public history; direct comparison of primary Spanish records with Tigua accounts and specialist scholarship; current, publicly authorized Tiwa language documentation; later legal developments; and a documented community-review process if one is ever undertaken.

A separate promising lead is Scott C. Comar’s 2015 UTEP dissertation, *The Tigua Indians of Ysleta del Sur: A Borderlands Community* (411 pages). The repository title page and abstract were inspected, but the download request returned HTTP 403. This is an automated-request result only: reader access requirements are undetermined, the full dissertation remains unread, and it does not count toward the source total. It is preserved as a research lead.

No historical map was added. The Texas regional route still describes only the present-day Pueblo/community location, not a homeland boundary. No private or generated imagery was used.
