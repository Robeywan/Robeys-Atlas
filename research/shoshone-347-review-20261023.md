# Shoshone source review — October 23, 2026

The Shoshone directory entry (original 347) now contains 12 distinct substantive works with actual content inspected across seven cited sections. One additional inspected page is retained as supporting context and excluded from the substantive count; one older NPS link remains an unread lead. Initial adequacy is **Still unfinished**. This broad access point is a selected introduction to multiple communities and governments, not a comprehensive history or one unified political identity.

## Scope and distinctions

The guide centers Idaho-connected sources from the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes at Fort Hall and the Shoshone-Paiute Tribes of Duck Valley. It also uses separate Eastern Shoshone and Northwestern Band sources to make clear that Shoshone histories and governments extend across present-day state borders. The name “Shoshone” does not denote one government. The sources use language and community labels in their own specific contexts; the NPS term “Shoshonean” is presented as the vocabulary of a particular Shoshone-Bannock collaboration, not as a universal community name.

The public synthesis covers identity and scope, Idaho seasonal places, community language/cultural programs, treaty text and later implementation, the Duck Valley government and its water settlement, and selected Northwestern and Eastern Shoshone histories. The map room was not expanded, and no map connection was added. WyoHistory contains a map reference, but its map was not independently inspected or assigned.

## Substantive works inspected

1. **Shoshone-Bannock Tribes, “About.”** Read the actual Culture and History section, especially its community composition, linguistic distinction, ancestral-land description and seasonal subsistence summary. This is the Fort Hall Tribes’ own account; it is not a boundary survey or universal Shoshone history.
2. **Shoshone-Bannock Language and Cultural Preservation Department, “Language and Cultural Preservation.”** Read the public sections on language instruction, archives, Original Territories research, oral histories, family history, museum work and sensitive-information stewardship. Changing class schedules were not carried forward.
3. **NPS with Shoshone-Bannock representatives, “Newenee: The Shoshonean Peoples of Southern Idaho.”** Read the complete exhibit page, including the Tribe’s welcome, language examples, artist credit, and elder quotation. The exhibit’s collaboration and authorship are stated in the annotation.
4. **NPS, “History — Craters of the Moon.”** Read the complete Shoshone-Bannock and Goodale’s Cutoff sections. The NPS account provides place-specific seasonal routes; its “limited resources” frame and settler-centered account of trail conflict are attributed rather than adopted as the only interpretation. Its public narratives are not repeated.
5. **Shoshone-Paiute Tribes of Duck Valley, Cultural Department, “Our History.”** Read the full historical narrative, including community composition, executive-order chronology, off-reservation movement, relocation resistance, schools and government description. The page’s undated acreage and membership figures are not used.
6. **U.S. Department of the Interior, “H.R. 1869: Technical Correction to the Shoshone-Paiute Tribes of the Duck Valley Reservation Water Rights Settlement Act of 2021.”** Read the complete 2021 statement for the record. This is a narrow federal account of the settlement, adjudications, and trust funds, not a full history or the Tribes’ own water narrative.
7. **Fort Bridger Treaty of 1868**, text reproduced by the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes from Charles J. Kappler’s 1904 compilation. Read the heading and Articles 1–2. The primary text distinguishes Eastern Shoshone and Bannock parties and sets out the separate-reservation provision; it does not establish how that provision was implemented.
8. **Shoshone-Bannock Tribes, Constitution and Bylaws.** Read the preamble and published territory, membership and council provisions. The page describes the Fort Hall government in an edition approved in 1936 and amended through 2013; later law and current practice require current verification.
9. **Eastern Shoshone Tribe, “About the Shoshone Tribe.”** Read the full public About page on the Wind River community, Washakie and Sacajawea. It is retained as the government’s own account. Broad archaeological and treaty-area figures were not independently checked and are not repeated as settled fact.
10. **WyoHistory.org, “Coming to Wind River: The Eastern Shoshone Treaties of 1863 and 1868.”** Read the complete article, including the treaty, migration and reservation sections, notes and bibliography. It is a cited secondary history useful for chronology and cross-checking, not a substitute for treaty text or the governments’ accounts.
11. **Mae Parry, “The Northwestern Shoshone,” in *A History of Utah’s American Indians* (2000).** Read selected pages from the open JSTOR chapter viewer: pp. 25–26, 34–42, 59–61 and 69–72. These pages address Northwestern Shoshone seasonal life, Bear River survivor history, Washakie settlement testimony and later legal history. The book introduction identifies the project as one proposed by Utah Indian leaders; Parry’s family and oral-history evidence is attributed. The entire 48-page chapter was not read.
12. **Nate Housley, Megan van Frank and Megan Weiss, “Lessons in Resilience,” produced for the Northwestern Band of the Shoshone Nation (Utah Humanities, 2024).** Read the full article, credits and source notes. It describes Mae Parry’s oral-history and archival work and the Band’s public history efforts. It gives a different Bear River death estimate than some other public summaries, so the page asserts no single figure.

## Research questions and unresolved differences

The Fort Bridger treaty text and the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes’ Treaty Day account differ from WyoHistory’s chronology for the relocation of Bannock people and Pocatello’s band to Fort Hall. The page keeps the sources’ dates attributed and does not merge the promise of a separate Bannock reservation with its later implementation.

Duck Valley, Fort Hall, Wind River and the Northwestern Band have distinct histories. The selected materials cannot represent all Shoshone governments, bands, families or language communities. More community-authored Nevada, Utah, Idaho and Wyoming materials, specialist language sources, family histories, and original treaty/claims records remain needed. No current population figure, exhaustive community list, consensus interpretation, or community endorsement is asserted. Quantity and initial adequacy remain separate; **Still unfinished** is the dated assessment.
