# Bannock (original ID 28) — source inspection and synthesis ledger

Reviewed October 19, 2026. Draft: `work/expanded-28.json`. Thirteen substantive sources were opened and inspected, not merely discovered in a bibliography. Eight sections contain approximately 820 words, with a 48-word overview. This is an expanded introduction with explicit gaps, not a completed or community-reviewed history.

## Scope decisions

- Distinguish Bannock people and the Bannock variety of Northern Paiute from Shoshoni and from the joint Shoshone-Bannock Tribes government. Shared institutions are clearly described as shared.
- Prioritize tribal publications and a tribal department’s signed guest essay, alongside two specialist articles. Multiple tribal publications count because they contribute distinct substantive content: treaty interpretation, archival/language work, a constitution, water governance, fisheries management, public horse traditions, and a dated energy plan.
- No current population or speaker count; no current officeholders or class times. The linguistic study’s dated speaker estimate was not copied into the profile.
- No image copied, ceremonial knowledge extracted, or restricted information reproduced. The preservation department explicitly describes its confidential and sensitive holdings.
- Paraphrases and source annotations stay below 200 words derived from any one webpage. Only the two-word treaty place-name transcription is quoted in the synthesis. Titles and institution names are identifiers.

## Inspected sources and evidence locations

1. **About / Culture and History**, Shoshone-Bannock Tribes, undated.
   - https://www.sbtribes.com/about/
   - Opened and read the reservation overview and “Culture and History”; web lines 83–127. Describes Bannock/Northern Paiute and Northern Shoshone composition, broad ancestral territory, related Numic languages, food gathering and hunting, horses, and reservation reduction.
   - Used for shared identity and land-loss overview. Does not assign every location in the joint ancestral territory exclusively to Bannock people. Migration dates are not independently adjudicated here.

2. **Bannock (Fort Hall, Idaho)**, Tim Thornes, *International Journal of American Linguistics* 86(S1), 2020, S13–S33.
   - https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/707225
   - Open full article. Introduction web lines 35–54; autobiography beginning lines 49–153 and following; recording notes lines 765–814. Bannock described as Northern Paiute variety with strong Shoshoni contact. Lizzie Edmo’s narrative recorded November 24, 1959, Fort Hall, by Sven Liljeblad; article explains transcription and analysis.
   - Used the introduction and the everyday travel/gathering/exchange material; identified speaker and recording context. Did not generalize an individual’s memories to all families, reproduce long language passages, or report the 2020 estimate as a present count.

3. **Embracing Tennambo’i**, Shoshone-Bannock Tribes Language and Cultural Preservation Department, hosted by NPS; updated September 25, 2024.
   - https://www.nps.gov/crmo/planyourvisit/embracing-tennambo-i.htm
   - Read full guest essay, web lines 21–36. Connects named landscapes, travel, food areas, public interpretation, invasive grass, and Centennial Marsh camas habitat work.
   - Indigenous authorship is explicit in source classification even though NPS hosts it. The essay’s public place-name discussion is contextualized rather than extracted as decoration.

4. **Treaty with the Eastern Band Shoshoni and Bannock, 1868**, tribal reproduction of Kappler’s 1904 compilation.
   - https://www.sbtribes.com/fort-bridger-treaty/
   - Read preamble and Articles 1–6, especially Article 2 (web lines 99–101) and Article 4 (108–110). Article 2 provides for a future Bannock reservation; text reads “Kansas Prairie,” whereas tribal interpretation identifies Camas Prairie. Profile now makes the discrepancy explicit.
   - Treaty-page metadata gives contradictory proclamation/ratification order; profile uses only the July 3, 1868 signing date. Need a manuscript / Statutes at Large comparison before stating corrected metadata. No present legal advice or comprehensive court interpretation.

5. **Shoshone-Bannock Tribes recognize Fort Bridger Treaty Day**, tribal public interpretation, no displayed date.
   - https://www.sbtribes.com/shoshone-bannock-tribes-recognize-fort-bridger-treaty-day/
   - Read full article, web lines 82–92. Describes the treaty as a living agreement; distinguishes promised Bannock reservation from actual relocation to Fort Hall in 1869 and joint governmental history.
   - This is an interpretive publication with different evidentiary work from the treaty itself. Ratification/proclamation metadata repeats the discrepancy noted above, so omitted those dates.

6. **Strengthening Relationships to Traditional Foodways: Adapting Food Practices Through Camas Cultivation Experiments on the Fort Hall Indian Reservation**, Sidney Fellows, Georgia Hart-Fredeluces, Nolan Brown, Morey Burnham, *Journal of Ethnobiology*, first online June 8, 2023.
   - https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/02780771231176361
   - Read abstract and study design; “Postcolonial Suppression” (web lines 68–78), “Contemporary Food Revitalization” (84–92), partnership methods (102–107), implications / partnership / conclusion (216–238), and permission footnote (249–251).
   - Study was developed with tribal participants, formal Business Council approval, and Culture Committee approval. Used the authors’ attributed historical synthesis connecting deprivation and 1878 war, plus contemporary cultivation and its acknowledged uncertainties. Did not count their unread bibliography as additional sources or present the historical section as a comprehensive military history.

7. **Language and Cultural Preservation**, tribal departmental page, undated.
   - https://www.sbtribes.com/language-culture/
   - Read program sections, web lines 85–172: director / sensitive information, archives, tribal-member instructors, original territories research, family histories, recordings and digital preservation, museum and Wrensted collection.
   - Used public institutional responsibilities and named language instruction. No reliance on personnel, schedules, website count widgets, or current visitor hours.

8. **Constitution and Bylaws**, constitution approved April 30, 1936, page states amendments through August 15, 2013.
   - https://www.sbtribes.com/constitution-bylaws/
   - Inspected preamble and territory/membership/governing-body/election provisions, web lines 87–132. Seven-member Fort Hall Business Council elected at large.
   - Web transcription repeats the Article II heading before council provisions. Source annotation flags this; precise legal quotation should use the linked official document. No independent determination of enrollment rules or their application.

9. **Indian Relay Racing**, tribal public account, undated.
   - https://www.sbtribes.com/relay-racing/
   - Read web lines 83–85 and 115–122: family transmission, four people / three horses, rider changes horses, holders and catcher.
   - Used the public mechanics and family continuity. Did not repeat the “over 500 years” chronology as established fact; source annotation explains origin claims as the Tribes’ account.

10. **Fish and Wildlife**, tribal program account, undated main text.
    - https://www.sbtribes.com/fish-and-wildlife/
    - Read mission, management authority, focus areas, ESA agreement, and Snake River policy (web lines 85–138 and 193–202).
    - Used fisheries stewardship and its connection to treaty exercise. Main text’s project descriptions are not treated as proof of completion; current event feed and staff counts excluded.

11. **Save Idaho Salmon**, tribal advocacy page referencing a 2021 congressional proposal.
    - https://www.sbtribes.com/saveidahosalmon/

Did not assert removal had been approved or completed, or independently endorse every numerical / scientific claim on this advocacy page. Embedded films were not counted as separately inspected sources.

12. **Tribal Water Resources Department and Commission**, tribal program account.

13. **Community Action Plan: Promoting Cleaner Air Through the Development of Renewable Energy Resources**, Tribal Department of Energy, November 15, 2022.
    - https://www.sbtribes.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/TDOE-Community-Action-Plan.pdf
    - Opened 36-page PDF; inspected title and letter; printed pp. 1–4, 10–14, 15–21, and 29–31. Main reading includes community survey summary, air monitoring context, energy measures, implementation sequence, conclusion.
    - Actual publication year 2022, not upload-path year 2024. Profile describes the plan and proposals, does not state project outcomes. No medical advice or pollutant exposure assertions derived from its general health discussion.
