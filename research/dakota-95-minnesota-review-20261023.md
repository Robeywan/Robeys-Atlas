# Dakota (entry 95) — Minnesota source supplement

Reviewed 2026-10-23. The public profile now contains **nine substantive inspected works**, seven supporting records excluded from that count, and ten cited sections. Its initial-adequacy judgment remains **Still unfinished**. This is a Minnesota-focused source guide, not a complete history of Dakota peoples, a comprehensive language study, a current demographic account, or a community-reviewed collection.

## New substantive works inspected

1. **Minnesota Tribal Government 2025 — Upper Sioux Community / Pezihutazizi Oyate profile.** Read the complete Upper Sioux profile on pp. 9–10 (PDF lines 347–381). The profile describes Pejuhutazizi Kapi as Dakota homeland, the community’s establishment in 1938, its 1995 constitution, and the reported return of 1,400 acres in 2024. The guide gives a total land base of 3,725 acres and a dated membership count of 577. The state guide carries the Community’s account; it is not independent validation of every historical statement. Its figures are tied to the 2025 publication and are not presented as current 2026 measurements or as a homeland boundary.
2. **Dakota Wicohan, Mni Sóta Maḳoce Curriculum public overview.** Read the instructor overview, goals, authorship, access/training notes, and ten-lesson outline (page lines 55–189). The Dakota-led curriculum covers homeland, land relationships, Dakota place names, sovereignty, treaty language, colonization, cultural and language continuity, and living together. The full lessons require teacher training; they were not accessed. The publisher says the curriculum was piloted in three school years and that student-outcome research was forthcoming. No effectiveness claim is made.
3. **Minnesota Historical Society, “Dakota Homeland” exhibition transcript.** Read the complete public text (lines 232–247). Dallas Ross, Grace Goldtooth-Campos, Clifford Canku, Walter LaBatte, and Raymond Owen discuss Dakota homeland, the Seven Council Fires, and relations with land and relatives. The transcript uses more than one formulation of Dakota identity. Statements remain attributed to their named speakers; this museum-selected transcript is not treated as a universal definition or community consensus. The linked video was not played.

## Supporting evidence, not source-count additions

- Minnesota DNR’s Upper Sioux Agency State Park closure/land-transfer page was read for the Community’s long-standing request, the park’s 1862 history, the 2024 closure, and the state’s explanation of the transfer (lines 5–15 and 31–35). It is a concise state-agency explanation, not a community history.
- Minnesota Laws 2023, Chapter 60, section 97, was read for the statutory direction to transfer state park land and the separate conditions on Minnesota Historical Society property (lines 4363–4372). It records state legal action, not the full history or oral understandings of the transfer.
- The Minnesota Indian Affairs Council’s undated Upper Sioux history page was read as an earlier community-attributed account (lines 16–36). It lists more than 900 acres and a population of 453, figures that differ from the 2025 state guide’s account of the 2024 return and dated count. These snapshots are not silently reconciled into a current figure. The direct Upper Sioux government site returned HTTP 403 to the request; the access control was not bypassed and the response is not described as a paywall.

## Synthesis and remaining gaps

The added records strengthen the profile’s coverage of Upper Sioux Community/People of Yellow Medicine, Dakota public interpretation and Dakota-created education. They also document a recent land return through both a community-attributed guide and state legal/agency records. The profile keeps the 2023 legal authorization separate from the 2025 guide’s report of the 2024 return, and it dates all acreage and membership data. It does not extend Upper Sioux Community history to all Dakota governments.

Further work should review the Upper Sioux Community constitution and direct public history or land-transfer materials when accessible; additional Dakota-authored historical scholarship and the underlying treaty records; specialist language scholarship; and distinct Dakota community sources from the Dakotas, Nebraska and Canada. The full Dakota Wicohan curriculum requires training access and no program outcomes were verified. No historical map association was added: this source pass did not inspect a specific map as an object or establish a new profile-to-map relationship.

Next regional focus: Minnesota’s distinct Ojibwe governments, beginning with sources that document community-specific names, governance, historical connections and contemporary public history. Then continue the Idaho queue. Maintain the source-first priority and add maps only when directly supported by the ordinary source review.
