# Dakota (entry 95) — Minnesota language and preservation supplement

Reviewed 2026-10-23. The profile now contains **12 substantive inspected works**, seven supporting references excluded from that count, and 12 cited sections. Its initial-adequacy judgment remains **Still unfinished**. This supplement strengthens the Minnesota-focused guide; it is not a complete history of Dakota peoples, a comprehensive language study, a current demographic account, or a community-reviewed collection.

## Three community sources inspected

1. **Prairie Island Indian Community, “Dakota Language.”** Read the complete visible page body, particularly its account of language suppression and renewal, community classes, culture camps and language-bowl activities, outreach at Red Wing High School, and educational exhibits at He Mni Can and the Grand Meadow Chert Quarry. These are the Community’s own descriptions of its programs and historical framing. The page still says a mobile application was expected in spring 2023; its current availability was not verified. External lesson, dictionary and language-institute links were not inspected.
2. **Shakopee Mdewakanton Sioux Community, “Dakota Language.”** Read the complete visible page. It describes the Community’s Dakota Iapi Típi, weekly classes, family learning events, youth culture camps, Dakota Language Bowl, keyboard and public learning work. Statements about language loss and recovery are retained as the Community’s own perspective. No speaker-count, language-classification or universal-program claim is inferred.
3. **Prairie Island Indian Community, “Cultural Preservation.”** Read the substantive page body describing the Community’s Tribal Historic Preservation Office, selected repatriation work, restoration of the public He Mni Can site and partnership around the Grand Meadow Chert Quarry. Broader archaeological claims remain attributed to the Community. Burial-specific details and sensitive locations were excluded from the synthesis; no coordinates or restricted material were accessed.

## What changed in the synthesis

The guide now gives readers distinct examples of language work at Prairie Island and Shakopee, rather than implying there is one Dakota-wide program. It also describes Prairie Island’s public-facing cultural-preservation work in the Community’s own terms. The sources add evidence about contemporary community institutions and education but do not independently establish language vitality, program outcomes, archaeology claims, or a shared practice across Dakota governments.

The quantitative target of twelve substantive works is now met, but **initial adequacy remains “Still unfinished.”** This Minnesota-oriented overview continues to have uneven depth across the four Minnesota Dakota governments and does not cover Dakota communities throughout the Dakotas, Nebraska, Canada or elsewhere. Specialist language scholarship, the Upper Sioux Community’s direct public sources, primary treaty and statutory records, and additional Dakota-authored history remain priorities. The current materials do not record community review or endorsement.

No historical map was added. This source review did not establish a new specific map-to-community relationship. The state-level map remains a discovery aid, not a homeland boundary.
