## Solís and Avery paragraph

The integrated section accurately uses the specific documents rather than treating the whole colonial site as an Adai village. Solís printed pp.64–65 locates the mission and presidio separately and says Indigenous people were numerous despite no mission congregation. The profile attributes baptism motives to settlers' reports and the inspector's suspicions instead of presenting them as Indigenous testimony. It does not repeat the disputed baptism total.

Avery printed p.80 explicitly explains omission from a 1766 Louisiana military list through Texas jurisdiction. The profile attributes the caution to Avery and leaves his interpretation of the unfilled 1757 map icon uncertain. It also avoids deriving ancestry from Spanish surnames. These are faithful, limited uses of the inspected text. A possible wording refinement, without changing the conclusion, would be “Avery explains the omission in terms of Texas jurisdiction,” making the historian's explanatory role even more explicit.

The research gap preserves the 103-versus-300 baptism disagreement. This is appropriate: Avery's printed p.77 gives 103, whereas the visually checked Kress translation on printed p.65 gives three hundred entries and continues with separate presidio records on p.66. The profile neither combines the figures nor presents baptism counts as a population census. It correctly avoids transferring the following Ays/Urjatana passage into Adai history.

## Texas Beyond History

Independently reopened the complete Cultural Worlds, Archival Research, and Legacy pages, with particular attention to the dedicated Adai discussion and the legacy distinctions. The profile's general description of waterway settlements, visits, trade, inconsistent naming, and living heritage has direct support. Its avoidance of precise territory boundaries is warranted: the exhibit itself contains both useful settlement evidence and an overconfident statement about fixing territory by the mission name.

The section's warning about older disappearance language is justified by Cultural Worlds, which says the tribe did not survive as a separate nation while elsewhere recognizing living Adai heritage. Legacy explicitly says the Adai Indian Nation still exists. The integrated text recognizes that tension rather than choosing extinction language as a verdict on current identity. It also does not conflate regional Adaeseño Spanish with Adaizan.

## Webb and Gregory state booklet

Independently read the Adaes section in the downloaded text layer, PDF pp.22–24, including its surrounding page transitions. This is a substantive dedicated discussion, not a passing name in a regional list. It considers kin-based dispersed hamlets, candidate site identifications, trade, pottery, community movement, and descendants. The integrated paragraph's wording about attributed archaeological hypotheses is supported.

The booklet's confident Caddoan-language assignment, problematic mission chronology, and claims about absorption are not imported as current settled conclusions. The profile does not infer the beginning of Adai people from a regional archaeological period or expose exact archaeological or funerary locations. Counting the booklet as one useful historical archaeology study is reasonable within these stated limits.

## Gregory, Avery, Lee and Blaine 2004

Independently viewed the actual scan at printed pp.65,67,71,74. The abstract and introduction frame cooperation and accommodation as the authors' interpretation. Excavation discussions and the conclusion connect documentary expectations with buildings, Indigenous ceramics, European goods and faunal remains in a mixed settlement. The integrated paragraph accurately presents this as interpretation and does not turn it into equal power, universal harmony, or proof that each vessel was made by an Adai person.

Printed p.71 discusses differentiated artifact distributions and possible households using expressly conditional language. The profile wisely avoids asserting a named ethnicity for those households. The wider presidio study is sufficiently relevant to the specific borderland relationship discussed, while its source annotation correctly marks its broader social scope.
