# Ojibwe (original entry 265) — Minnesota government and community review

Reviewed 2026-10-23. The public directory profile now contains **13 substantive inspected works**, eight supporting references excluded from the substantive count, and seven cited sections. Initial adequacy is **Still unfinished**. This is a Minnesota-focused introductory source guide, not an exhaustive history of Ojibwe peoples, a complete review of every Minnesota community, a universal account of Ojibwe identity, or community endorsement.

## New substantive works inspected

1. **Mille Lacs Band, “History.”** Read the full public history and self-government sections. The Band’s account connects its relationship with central Minnesota to its 1837/1855 treaties, later land loss and boarding schools, and reconstruction of government in the mid-twentieth century. A linked book-length Social History was not opened or counted.
2. **Mille Lacs Band, “Treaties.”** Read the full sections on the 1837 and 1855 treaties and current practice. The Band explains retained harvesting rights, the 1994/1997 litigation, the 1999 Supreme Court result and its conservation code. This is the Band’s explanation, not a substitute for the full treaty or court opinions.
3. **Mille Lacs Band, *The Non-Removable Guide*.** Inspected pages 2–5, including a community migration account, food practices, treaty summaries, the Sandy Lake tragedy and removal/allotment history. These passages remain attributed to the Band and are not treated as independently verified treaty interpretation.
4. **Red Lake Nation, “Tribal History & Historical Photos.”** Read the visible seventeenth- through twentieth-century timeline. It describes Red Lake’s account of movement into northern Minnesota, the Old Crossing treaty, allotment pressure and Red Lake’s separate political path from the six-band Minnesota Chippewa Tribe. The embedded photo video was not played.
5. **White Earth Nation Cultural Division, “Culture.”** Read the Division’s mission, language/cultural-program overview and brief account of naming and White Earth Reservation’s 1867 establishment. The naming discussion is the Nation’s own usage and is not a universal definition for all communities.
6. **White Earth Nation, *Climate Action Plan* (2025).** Inspected pages 2–3 and 12–14: reservation, land/cultural context, 1867 history, governance, collaborative planning methods and survey scope. This is a contemporary community plan, not a general history. Its survey respondents and modeled outcomes remain tied to the plan.
7. **Bois Forte Band, “A Brief Summary of Bois Forte History.”** Read the complete public history. It distinguishes the Nett Lake, Vermilion and Deer Creek sectors, presents the Band’s migration account, summarizes 1854 and 1866 treaty history and an 1881 executive order, and describes cultural and community continuity.
8. **Bois Forte Heritage Center & Cultural Museum, About and Exhibits pages.** Read both community pages. They describe the Center’s origin through elder and Tribal Council Member Phyllis Boshey, the Band’s migration account, exhibits, and the public list of oral-history interviews. The interview transcripts themselves were not read; sacred items are not reproduced.
9. **National Park Service, Jeffrey Richner, “Voyageurs National Park.”** Read the full research summary. It combines archaeological work, photographs, historical records and oral histories to document Bois Forte occupation in or near the present park, especially in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The author explicitly limits the evidence for tracing groups further back.
10. **National Park Service, “History & Culture — Grand Portage National Monument.”** Read the complete page. It identifies the monument as within the homeland of the Grand Portage Anishinaabe and describes Gichi Onigaming, the Band’s contribution of land to the monument and Minong (Isle Royale) as a traditional cultural property for Grand Portage. The interpretation is geographically specific.
11. **Fond du Lac Band, “Ojibwemowin.”** Read the full language statement. The Band describes its 2010 resolution naming Ojibwe as its official language and the creation of a 2015 language program. The page is a community perspective, not a current speaker count.

The pre-existing Sault Tribe language page and Smithsonian “Leech Lake Ojibwe — Meet the People” lesson were also read in full for their visible substantive sections and retained as two additional works. The Sault page is one Michigan-based community’s regional language perspective; the Smithsonian module is a Leech Lake-specific educational starting point. No linked videos or teaching materials were counted as inspected.

## Synthesis and limits

The regional inventory names seven Minnesota Ojibwe governments: Bois Forte, Fond du Lac, Grand Portage, Leech Lake, Mille Lacs, Red Lake and White Earth. The six member Bands of the Minnesota Chippewa Tribe share a constitution but retain distinct governments; Red Lake Nation has its own separate political history. This profile now points to each of these seven access routes without claiming equal depth. Community pages and nation-specific sources are read in their own scope. Ojibwe, Chippewa and Anishinaabe are retained as names used by different communities and sources, not collapsed into a single governmental identity.

Several profiles are still thin at this level: Leech Lake is represented here by an educational module; Fond du Lac by a language-program statement and an older state-hosted history; Grand Portage by its government page and NPS place interpretation. The Mille Lacs treaty pages, White Earth contemporary plan and Bois Forte research/heritage materials provide richer but still distinct evidence. The older Fond du Lac state narrative is not used to substantiate broad cultural claims.

The Minnesota Chippewa Tribe’s linked “History of the Tribe” page did not expose substantive text in this inspection, and the 2015 Leech Lake annual report exceeded the source tool’s document-size limit. The linked Mille Lacs Social History, Bois Forte oral-history transcripts and Smithsonian video transcript remain unread. These are inspection constraints, not claims that a reader paywall exists; no limit was bypassed.

Further work should add direct community historical materials for every Minnesota Band, primary treaty records and legal decisions, specialist Ojibwe-language scholarship, relevant Wisconsin and Canadian communities, and additional community-reviewed public history. The existing Grand Portage map-profile association is retained with its prior evidence; this pass did not inspect a map object or create a new map association. The state-level regional route remains a navigation aid, not a homeland boundary.

## Leech Lake Band follow-up — October 23, 2026

Added p265-s23, the Leech Lake Band of Ojibwe’s *Materials Related to the Co-Management of the Chippewa National Forest*. The source is a 133-page compilation. I inspected the full 20-page historical/current overview titled “The Right to Self-Governance” (PDF pages 4–23), but did not inspect the later presentations, MOU, appendices, management letters, implementation guide, or species list as separate works. The Band’s website policy identifies the site as its official site and says content is submitted by Band officials and staff.

This first-party account expands the Leech Lake reading path beyond the Smithsonian lesson. It explains the Band’s framing of treaty-era land changes, the Nelson Act, allotment and logging, the 1898 Sugar Point conflict, and its relationship with the Chippewa National Forest. It records different Pillager and military accounts of how the 1898 conflict began. The booklet’s treaty-right and legal conclusions remain attributed; treaty texts, court opinions and cited historical records need separate review. Its bundled 2019–2020 materials are dated institutional documents, not a status audit of current policy. No map or sensitive location information was reused.

## Leech Lake judicial opinions follow-up — October 23, 2026

Added p265-s24 and p265-s25 after reading the complete reported opinions reproduced by Justia: *Leech Lake Band of Chippewa Indians v. Herbst*, 334 F. Supp. 1001 (D. Minn. 1971), and *State v. Forge*, 262 N.W.2d 341 (Minn. 1977), including the opinions’ notes. Together they clarify that the 1971 federal district court recognized treaty rights to hunt, fish, and gather wild rice on public reservation lands and waters, but did not recognize the Band’s asserted exclusive regulatory authority. The 1977 Minnesota Supreme Court then reviewed a 1973 negotiated/statutory fishing-stamp compromise, recognized the retained rights and upheld the legal arrangement in the specific case before it. These dated holdings are not current regulatory advice. Treaty originals, trial exhibits, the settlement document, subsequent case law and current regulatory texts were not independently checked.
