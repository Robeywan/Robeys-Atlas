# Adai historical and linguistic supplement

Inspected 2026-10-20. No main data, build, Site, or publication was edited. The companion JSON contains four recommended core resources and two optional historical-classification works. All six have dedicated substantive Adai passages and are cited in proposed prose; no brief name list or bibliographic record has been counted. Root may omit either optional work without weakening the minimum collection.

## ad-history-1: Sibley's 1805 account, inspected in an 1808 reprint

Actual scan downloaded and relevant text read: `work/adai-american-state-papers.pdf`, 190 PDF pages. The title page (PDF p. 5) identifies American State Papers, Boston: Munroe, Francis, & Parker, 1808. The contents and printed pp. 42–44 (PDF pp. 50–52) identify and reproduce Sibley's Historical Sketches. The complete Adaize paragraph is on printed p. 44, PDF p. 52.

<https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/79/American_state_papers_%28IA_americanstatepap01unit%29.pdf#page=52>

Read the adjacent Caddoques and Yattassees paragraphs for context. The specific Adai entry separates the community at Lac Macdon from the old Spanish establishment to its south, gives an estimate of men plus more women, and distinguishes their own language from additional Caddo and French usage. These are attributed statements by Sibley, not independently verified census or language-family findings. The account is the 1805 report included with Jefferson's 1806 communication, but the actual edition inspected is the 1808 reprint. It is not the familiar 1832 American State Papers Indian Affairs volume, despite the repeated series title.

The scan's extracted text has spacing and other OCR problems, including in the Natchez sentence. No Natchez date or claim of universal successful/unsuccessful conversion is adopted. An attempt to extract a page image returned a background layer rather than a fully rendered text page, so that extraction is not represented as a successful visual collation. The substantive paragraph was read from the PDF text and compared with its quotation in Swanton, not verified against the inaccessible 1806 edition.

## ad-history-2: Sibley's separate 1807 journal, edited 1922

Actual 112-page PDF downloaded: `work/adai-sibley-1807.pdf`.

<https://archive.org/download/reportfromnatchi00sibl/reportfromnatchi00sibl.pdf>

Bibliographic repository record (metadata, not a separate counted source):
<https://library.si.edu/digital-library/book/reportfromnatchi00sibl>

Read printed pp. 5–8 of Abel's introduction and pp. 49–56 of the journal, covering August 11–18, 1807. Printed p. 50 (PDF p. 52), August 13, names parties of Adaize along with chiefs of the enumerated nations, then discusses provisions and the practical difficulties of the large gathering. Page 51's provisioning list is not a comprehensive census. **The August 18 formal council list on p. 56 does not name Adai.** The proposed paragraph therefore establishes a recorded visit and interaction, not a treaty signature, an individual speaker's speech, or attendance at every later session.

The introduction and the journal contain period hierarchies and stereotypes. The agent's administrative categories and the editor's later appraisal are distinct voices. Hietan flag exchanges and other groups' speeches are not relabeled as Adai acts. The text's ceremonial descriptions are not reproduced in proposed prose. The 1807 journal is a distinct document from the 1805 sketch, despite shared authorship.

## ad-history-3: Swanton's 1942 Caddo sourcebook

Actual full-text transcription read in selected passages:

<https://louisiana-anthology.org/303_download/texts/swanton--caddo/swanton--caddo.html>

Title pages identify Source Material on the History and Ethnology of the Caddo Indians, Bureau of American Ethnology Bulletin 132, Washington, 1942. **This is not the Lower Mississippi Valley book, which dates to 1911.**

Selected inspected passages: pp. 13–15 on naming, remembered communities, and Caddo informants; pp. 21–23 on discrepant Adai population observations and tabulation; p. 75's quoted Sibley material; pp. 84–85 on land claims and the uncertain identity of a village called Adaise. Swanton records different witnesses and dates, sometimes retaining uncertainty and sometimes imposing his own classification. The population table explicitly distinguishes warriors from totals and calculates some totals by multiplying warriors by four. It must not be converted into a uniform census series.

The land-record discussion provides an unusually useful explicit warning: a later place called Adaise might not designate an Indigenous village. The name is not sufficient evidence by itself. His complete-merger conclusion and the dismissive term applied to Adai and Eyeish are period authorial judgments. The supplement does not repeat them as contemporary identity findings. Broader descriptions of Caddo architecture, religion, or customs elsewhere in the book were not read as evidence of specifically Adai practice.

## ad-history-4: Mithun's modern linguistic analysis

Actual author-posted chapter full text inspected through ResearchGate, not merely its abstract:

<https://www.researchgate.net/publication/345108369_Language_Isolates_of_North_America>

The page identifies Marianne Mithun as the author and uploader, chapter pp. 193–228, publication October 2017, DOI 10.4324/9781315750026-8. Read the opening discussion on pp. 193–195 and the Adai paragraph on p. 195. The distinction between isolate and unclassifiable language depends partly on quantity and quality of documentation. In the Adai paragraph, Mithun reviews the surviving vocabulary and Gatschet's comparison, reports Chafe's rejection of identification as Caddo, and argues that an isolate is suggested.

Do not treat an unpublished personal communication cited by Mithun as independently inspected evidence. Do not adopt the chapter's compressed mission dates or early historical identifications for the chronology; these need the separate mission/history research. Its value here is the specialist's explicit linguistic reasoning and documentation caveat. The proposed prose does not say that scholarly classification invalidates Adai Caddo identity or contemporary language work.

## ad-history-5: Powell's 1891 classification account — optional

Read the complete Adaizan family discussion, printed pp. 45–47, in the Project Gutenberg transcription of the Smithsonian report:

<https://www.gutenberg.org/files/17286/17286-h/17286-h.htm#page45>

It supplies a substantial dedicated discussion, including earlier bibliographic naming, a reported Gatschet comparison, and the explicit admission that evidence sufficient to establish a Caddoan relationship was unavailable. The paragraph about not finding speakers is a limitation of the investigator's search, not proof that a people no longer existed. Mission dates, a proposed Cabeza de Vaca identification, emigrant-family claims, and a later Sibley date need independent primary checks and are not adopted.

This provides intellectual history distinct from a modern classification statement. It overlaps substantially with the materials behind the other works, so the resource is offered optionally and should not be used to manufacture a count of independent witnesses.

## ad-history-6: Gatschet's 1884 discussion — optional

Read the dedicated Adai section on printed pp. 41–42 and the following Caddo list on pp. 43–44, in the full transcription:

<https://www.gutenberg.org/files/49031/49031-h/49031-h.htm>

The older author describes remembered social affiliation, warns about the vocabulary's orthography, and offers limited comparative suggestions. The Caddo list is explicitly attributed to an unnamed Caddo person in 1882. The text's attribution of a manuscript vocabulary to Martin Duralde differs from later summaries emphasizing Sibley; no resolution is claimed without inspecting the actual holdings. The observations are not proof of an established modern family classification. Its importance is explaining the development and uncertainties of the historical argument.

Only those Adai-related pages were read for this subtask. The Creek migration legend, religious material, and broad period stereotypes elsewhere are outside the proposed material. As with Powell, this optional source adds a dedicated historical discussion, not another independent observation of all the reported events.

## Remaining work and integration

The strongest collection needs contemporary community voices, actual records of the mission and presidio, and specialist archaeology alongside these documents. Identity, legal recognition, language genealogy, mission affiliation, and a place bearing the name Adaes are separate questions. The four core resources can support the main profile; Powell and Gatschet should stay in optional contextual reading unless their distinct historical argument is used substantively. No conclusions about current recognition, family enrollment, or unbroken individual genealogies have been inferred from the inspected material.
