# Kootenai (Ktunaxa): Idaho source review — October 23, 2026

Two inspected works strengthen the Idaho-centered Kootenai profile: a 2003 congressional-hearing statement by Gary Aitken Sr., then chairman of the Kootenai Tribe of Idaho, and a 2025 U.S. Geological Survey technical report prepared in cooperation with the Tribe. The profile now has eight substantive works, seven synthesis sections, and an explicit **Still unfinished** initial-adequacy judgment. No map was added.

## Sources inspected

**Gary Aitken Sr., statement on behalf of the Upper Columbia United Tribes, U.S. Senate Committee on Indian Affairs hearing, June 4, 2003.** The official searchable hearing PDF was downloaded from Congress.gov. I read printed pages 14–16 (PDF pages 18–20), including all of Aitken’s oral statement. He identifies himself as the Kootenai Tribe of Idaho chairman and UCUT vice-chairman, describes the Kootenai Valley Resource Initiative around Bonners Ferry, and discusses white sturgeon, burbot, fish and wildlife management, and the effects he attributes to Libby Dam. The testimony is one dated coalition policy statement by an identified Kootenai leader; it does not speak for all Ktunaxa communities or provide an independent ecological evaluation. The separate prepared-statement appendix was not inspected or counted. [Official hearing PDF](https://www.congress.gov/108/chrg/CHRG-108shrg87846/CHRG-108shrg87846.pdf).

**Megan K. Kenworthy and Taylor J. Dudunake, *Effects of Restoration Work on Kootenai River White Sturgeon Critical Habitat, 2011–22* (USGS Scientific Investigations Report 2025–5071).** The actual 46-page PDF was downloaded from the USGS. I read the abstract, introduction pages 3–5, discussion page 28, and summary/conclusions page 30; title, acknowledgments, and contents were checked. The report identifies the restoration program as Kootenai Tribe-led and describes the study’s physical scope. It reports increased pool and depth measures after construction, while saying the tested conditions did not meet the project's initial channel-depth target. Sediment concentrations declined at two monitoring sites, but the authors conclude the pattern points to reduced incoming supply rather than restoration treatments materially changing sediment transport within the study reach. The report calls for longer monitoring to separate year-to-year variability from channel adjustment. Methods, complete results, associated data release, and figures were not reviewed. The publication landing page returned an automated 403, but the linked USGS PDF downloaded normally; this is not described as a reader paywall. The study is a bounded technical assessment, not an evaluation of the Tribe’s whole stewardship program or Ktunaxa culture. [USGS publication record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/sir20255071) · [USGS report PDF](https://pubs.usgs.gov/sir/2025/5071/sir20255071.pdf) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3133/sir20255071).

## Synthesis and limits

The two works offer complementary evidence: Aitken’s dated community-government perspective documents how the Tribe publicly framed local fisheries and shared resource management in 2003; the USGS report evaluates selected habitat metrics in one stretch of river, with the Tribe identified as project lead and research partner. The profile attributes each perspective and finding. It does not turn the 2003 policy statement into independent ecological proof or treat the technical report as a complete history.

The added research leaves the profile unfinished. It remains uneven across the separate Canadian First Nations and Montana Ksanka/Kootenai community; the 2025 study does not fill those gaps. The Tribe’s 2009 restoration master plan, the complete methods/results and data release, specialist language-learning work, and additional community perspectives remain open. No community endorsement or comprehensive history is claimed.
