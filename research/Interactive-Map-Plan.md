# Interactive map for Robey’s Atlas of Indigenous North America

Recorded October 19, 2026; expanded October 20, 2026 from the owner’s detailed vision.

The eventual atlas should be map-focused, with the map prominent in its main experience. It will provide a geographical route into the existing directory. Visitors should be able to move around North America, zoom from a continental view into a locality, select a place or area, see the relevant nations and peoples, and open their researched profiles and sources. The desired movement is smooth and exploratory, similar to the experience of navigating Google Earth.

## The owner’s example: Kansas and a local town

A visitor asks: “I live in Kansas. Who are the Native Americans in my area?” They select the general area on the map or search for their town, zoom to a useful local scale, and receive a readable list of the peoples and nations associated with that place. Each result explains whether it concerns a historical homeland, a particular settlement or period, displacement or migration, or a present-day community, and links to the existing directory profile, local history and evidence.

This should work as a general place-to-people learning path, without requiring visitors to know a tribal name first. Present state borders help orient the visitor but do not define Indigenous homelands. Search and approximate clicking should both work. Missing map evidence should be stated plainly. No Kansas-specific locations or nations are asserted by this planning example; those features require their own research.

## Visitor experience

1. Open a map centered on North America, covering the geographical scope of the directory.
2. Pan and zoom using a mouse, touch gestures, keyboard controls, and clearly labeled buttons. Consider rotation, tilt, terrain, and smooth flights as the experience develops.
3. Click an area or a location marker to open a readable list of associated peoples and communities. Multiple entries can appear for the same area.
4. Select a name to see a concise identification, the map feature’s time period and source, and an **Open profile** link to that entry’s existing directory URL.
5. Search for a nation or place. A nation search should highlight its documented locations and offer the same profile link. A profile should also have a **View on map** link when suitable map evidence exists.
6. Retain an accessible list view and the existing directory so the map remains an additional way to explore the research.

## Historical and contemporary context

Separate ancestral homelands, documented historical places, and present-day communities or governmental locations through clear layers and dates. A present administrative seat is a point of reference, not the entire homeland. Let overlapping or shared areas show multiple entries; distinguish related peoples, individual governments, umbrella groupings, historical towns, and aliases.

Each feature needs identifiable evidence: source URLs, the people or community represented, the feature’s meaning, a relevant date or period, review status, and geographical precision. Communicate approximate or uncertain extents. An absence of map data means research is missing, rather than that no Indigenous people belong to the area. Do not invent precise territorial boundaries from broad regional descriptions or plot a regional midpoint as an established community location.

Use publicly shareable locations. Sensitive cultural places and private or restricted sites do not belong in the public map dataset. Check permissions, attribution, and reuse terms for geographical datasets, basemaps, terrain, satellite imagery, and photographs before implementation.

## Preparation during the research phase

Continue researching the entries in alphabetical order, with A entries first and the revised quality-based source target: aim for twelve excellent inspected works, allowing a smaller collection such as five or six when careful research and a documented coverage review support it. When a source provides useful geographical evidence, save its location, time period, scope, and citation alongside the profile for later mapping. Geography research should strengthen the article and map evidence, rather than delay the whole A cohort to draw boundaries prematurely.

Source collections across the directory come first; the owner has explicitly allowed this to take weeks or months. The map has been requested as a later feature. It has not been implemented or published. Library, imagery provider, hosting requirements, account access, licensing, costs, and maintenance will be evaluated when implementation begins; no Google product integration has been selected or promised.

## First prototype acceptance

- Smooth pan and zoom work on desktop and touch devices.
- A place-first journey works without knowing a nation’s name: region or state, town/search or approximate selection, associated nations, then profiles and sources.
- A small, carefully sourced set of example locations supports click-to-list and list-to-profile navigation.
- Overlapping associations return more than one entry where the evidence warrants it.
- Historical and present-day features are clearly distinguished, with sources and dates visible.
- Profile links preserve the existing directory routes; search, keyboard access, and a usable list alternative work.
- Review the prototype with the owner before expanding it across the directory or adding more elaborate globe and terrain controls.
