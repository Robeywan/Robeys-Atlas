## Purpose and audience

Create an educational atlas that a college student or a general reader can use comfortably. Help someone begin with a familiar place, discover the Indigenous peoples and nations connected to it, and follow clear explanations into reliable sources. Favor simple navigation, readable language, traceable facts and explicit uncertainty. Editorial opinion and unsupported assertions do not serve as factual evidence. Clearly attribute historical interpretation, oral histories and first-person experiences rather than presenting one account as universal fact.

## A distinct visual setting for each profile

Each nation’s page should eventually use photographs selected specifically for that community: its people, basketry, food, music, artwork, clothing, landscapes and publicly shared cultural life where appropriate evidence and rights exist. For example, an Abenaki page should use verified Abenaki imagery, rather than generic decoration transferred from another people.

Record the photographer or artist, subject identification, context, date, credit, license or permission, and cultural restrictions. Distinguish historical photographs from present-day life. Public availability alone does not authorize reuse. Exclude restricted or sensitive material; do not turn ceremonial designs into generic ornaments. Preserve substantial readable space for the article and sources even when the surrounding page is visually rich.
