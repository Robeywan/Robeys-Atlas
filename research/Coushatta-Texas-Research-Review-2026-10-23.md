## Reviewed evidence and section use

- **Tribal constitution (amended through 2023):** the official 25-page document was inspected through its preamble and Articles I–V (printed pages 3–8), with selected amendment notes through page 23. It states that Alabama and Coushatta were formerly distinct tribes and that the Texas government is one federally recognized tribe for legal/governmental purposes; it describes territory and government organization. Detailed membership provisions are not reproduced. Used in “Federal law changed the Texas government’s legal relationship.”
- **1954 Public Law 83-627, 68 Stat. 768:** both scanned pages and sections 1–8 were read in GovInfo. It provides for transfer of federal trust lands to Texas and termination of federal supervision/services following a proclamation specified by the act. The proclamation and implementation record were not reviewed. The statute is not treated as the end of the people or Tribe. The official Statutes at Large front matter identifies the act at page 768 as Public Law 83-627; page-header/OCR extraction around page 769 can be confusing.
- **1987 Public Law 100-89, Title II:** all of Title II, sections 201–206 (printed pages 669–673), was read in the seven-page Statutes at Large PDF. Title II restores federal recognition, the federal trust relationship and services for the Texas Alabama-Coushatta government and treats the two historic tribes as one legal unit for federal-law purposes. Title I concerns Ysleta del Sur Pueblo and is not applied to Coushatta. No current legal advice or later case-law analysis is offered.
- **1836 treaty transcription:** the opening and proposed land article of the February 23, 1836 treaty were read in the edited *Indian Papers of Texas and the Southwest*, volume I, pages 14–15. The text names Alabama and Coushatta among groups styled “associate Bands” and proposes a single community within described bounds. It is a Republic of Texas diplomatic text in its own colonial/state framing. Neither the transcript nor the volume’s scan establishes ratification, implementation, Indigenous acceptance, or a present boundary. The displayed site is the archival volume route; its manuscript original and signatures were not reviewed.
- **Harriet Smither (1932), “The Alabama Indians of Texas”:** selected actual pages of the 27-page PDF were read, including the article’s early migration discussion, 1850s land/reservation account and reports concerning later federal land actions through the 1928 purchase. The entire article and cited archival documents were not independently checked. It is older outside scholarship with period terminology and paternalistic language; no historical population figures or character judgments are carried forward as current fact. JSTOR terms are printed in the scan; no text or images are reproduced.
- **Texas Historical Commission (February 24, 2026), “The History of the Alabama-Coushatta and Their Role in the Texas Revolution”:** complete article body and image captions were read. It draws on named secondary works, a state resolution, treaty records, and an attributed statement by Chief Colita; it says the Texas Senate declined to ratify the treaty. This is a state-agency educational and commemorative retrospective, not a community-authored account or independent corroboration of every cited claim. Underlying books, resolution, manuscript and image rights were not independently reviewed.

## Synthesis, distinctions, and limits

The page separates political and peoplehood categories. The 1987 statute and current Texas constitution describe a shared legal/governmental unit for the Alabama-Coushatta Tribe of Texas; the constitution also calls Alabama and Coushatta formerly distinct tribes. This legal arrangement does not collapse Alabama identity into Coushatta identity or make the Texas, Louisiana, and Oklahoma governments interchangeable.

The 1836 document is presented as a proposal with an attributed later account of non-ratification. The map-like boundary wording is not used as a homeland claim. The 1954 and 1987 laws are described from their enacted text, with the 1954 proclamation and subsequent implementation left unresolved. No historical map was added because no map-to-profile connection was reviewed in this batch.
