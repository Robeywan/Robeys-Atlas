# Coahuiltecan profile review — October 22, 2026

## Scope and judgment

This is a Texas-focused introductory profile for a broad historical umbrella label associated with South Texas and northeastern Mexico. It does not treat “Coahuiltecan” as one nation, government, language, or uniform culture. The page distinguishes named historical groups and local mission settings from the separate contemporary accounts published by Tāp Pīlam Coahuiltecan Nation and the Miakan/Garza Band through Indigenous Cultures Institute.

Twelve works were substantively inspected and three references were reviewed but excluded from that count. The initial judgment is **Still unfinished**. Reaching the flexible source target does not resolve the breadth of this umbrella entry, close the record gaps, or represent community review or endorsement.

## Main findings and limits

* The Texas State Historical Association describes hundreds of separately named groups and debates over a larger linguistic/cultural classification. Texas Beyond History explicitly uses Coahuiltecan as a geographic catch-all and names Payaya, Pacuache and Mariames while emphasizing linguistic and social diversity. These are syntheses about a region, not an exhaustive people-by-people record.
* The origin of the term is disputed in the inspected material. A 2014 CHAPS chapter describes “Coahuiltecan” as a later scholarly category; the current San Antonio Missions National Park page says Spanish colonists formed the term from the regional name. The reviewed sources do not settle this. The profile presents both attributions and adopts neither as uncontested fact.
* Mission records are incomplete. Campbell and Campbell’s 1985 study reports inconsistent registers and uncertainty matching recorded names to earlier places and languages. The Espíritu Santo exhibit describes Aranama, Tamiques and Manos de Prieta in that mission context and notes disagreement over Aranama/Mariame identification. No mission list is treated as a complete homeland map.
* Tāp Pīlam’s public timeline is identified as that Nation’s own account. Indigenous Cultures Institute describes its Miakan/Garza Band founders and public programs. A 2023 peer-reviewed article examines one Miakan-Band Elders’ educational encounter; it is not a pan-regional cultural survey. These are distinct community sources and cannot be collapsed into a single representative voice.
* The Institute’s language page documents a reclamation program chronology but contains an internally contradictory claim (“extinct” while fewer than 1,000 people supposedly speak the language). The profile does not repeat the number or infer current language vitality. A 2024 INAH article was inspected only at abstract/metadata level and remains excluded.
* Older scholarly writing uses extinction and cultural-loss language. The page attributes those interpretations and does not infer disappearance of descendants from missing names, language documentation, or sparse records. The 2001 NPS report discusses different views in one mission context; it does not establish present legal status.

No current population, exhaustive descendant list, present legal-status determination, or specific map association is asserted. No map was examined as part of this source batch. Community review remains open.
