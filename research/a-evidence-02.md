## Material findings

The old guide left a specific community-authorized continuity source as a gap. A public marker at Choccolocco Park supplies a substantial improvement: it identifies the speakers as Arbeka (Abihka), calls the location their ancestors’ ceremonial ground, and describes removal and carrying the sacred fire west. Muscogee Media independently reports the Muscogee Nation’s 2022 return festival at the original Arbeka grounds. The public 2023 archaeological report credits Arbeka elder Arnold Taylor and preservation staff; it compares Jim Fife’s pre-removal Choccolocco records with a 1927 affidavit by his grandson Dorsey Fife, who identified his grandfather with Arbeka. The profile reports this as the report’s evidence and conclusion, with original archival verification left open.

Do not infer a separate modern national government, a current leadership list, an unbroken institution at one physical site, or sameness among every settlement called Abihka/Arbeka/Abihkutci. Swanton specifically distinguishes two Abihkutci names and discusses abandoned Abihka grounds alongside continuing Talladega. His 1931 Abihka/Talladega discussion must be read at its actual early twentieth-century dates.

## Included sources: actual inspections and limits

1. **Public Muscogee (Creek) Nation / Arbeka (Abihka) marker, 2016.** https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=144923 . Read full inscription and editorial metadata. HMdb is a third-party transcription/photo host, while the inscription itself speaks in the Arbeka first person and is headed Muscogee (Creek) Nation. Marker erection credited to City of Oxford; photos dated January 26, 2020. No claim that this agent separately inspected the physical marker, heard an audio recording, or obtained community review. This primary public interpretation establishes an ancestral relationship without becoming a full genealogy.
2. **Braden Harper, Mvskoke Media, April 20, 2022, “MCN makes trek to homelands in Alabama.”** https://www.mvskokemedia.com/mcn-makes-trek-to-homelands-in-alabama/ . Full article read. Specific Arbeka ancestral location, Reyicepes return festival, leaders’ interpretation of removal and healing, school partnerships, event activities. Do not count the newspaper PDF mirror as another source. Future partnerships and aspirations not described as completed outcomes.
3. **Robert E. Perry & Associates, public Choccolocco Creek (Davis Farm) report, March 31, 2023.** https://assets.caboosecms.com/media/15086003_investigations-at-the-choccolocco-creek-archaeological-complex-public-document.pdf . Actual PDF downloaded into memory and pypdf text inspected, not merely its search excerpt. 166 PDF pages. Read preface i–v, relevant printed pp. 57–65, and 69–73. Most directly relevant: printed 60–61 (PDF72–73) on divergent town classifications, Jim Fife and Dorsey Fife; preface credits Taylor and Muscogee staff and acknowledges preliminary character. Public report is only one of three technical volumes, so no claim all volumes read. Later discussion of catastrophes, clan symbolism, and very ancient continuity contains hypotheses; the profile does not turn them into consensus. Readings should not expose archaeological coordinates or ceremonial recipes.

Canonical record opened and its actual PDF link discovered: https://repository.si.edu/bitstreams/e11ed50f-4a96-4db7-92a9-47d13d3c0e9b/download (36.33 MB). Old stored direct URL returned404. 5. **Benjamin Hawkins, Sketch (1798–99), first published1848.** https://dlg.usg.edu/record/g-hi_g-hiia_collectionsofgeo31hawk . Actual Digital Library of Georgia full OCR read, especially printed24–25 and41–42. Gives Au-be-coo-che settlement and landscape, identifies informant Co-tau-lau, and reports broad use of Au-be-cuh among elders. OCR introduces errors; precise quotations should use page scans. Hawkins was a federal agent promoting agricultural and political agendas; his account is a dated encounter, not unmediated community description. Its statements about marriage do not become current norms.
6. **Alabama Indigenous Mound Trail / University of Alabama Museums, Choccolocco site interpretation.** https://alabamamoundtrail.org/mound-site/choccolocco/ . Entire relevant interpretive text read. Provides accessible chronological overview of distinct mound-building phases and the later Abihka ceremonial association, with qualified flood-remembrance interpretation. Included as a distinct public interpretive resource beside the technical report, not a mirror or independent confirmation of every interpretation. Does not prove an unchanged named Abihka polity existed at the site for12,000years.
7. **Treaty with the Creeks, March24,1832, NMAI transcript.** https://americanindian.si.edu/nk360/removal-muscogee/transcription-creek-treaty.html . Full transcript read, especially1–5,12–14. The precise reason for including national context is the census/land-selection framework used in the Choccolocco archival record. This is not a separately named Abihka treaty. Article12 expressly says people were free to go or stay; later forced removal is therefore distinguished from the treaty’s promises. No present legal advice or conclusion on current rights asserted.
8. **Swanton, Modern Square Grounds of the Creek Indians, November11,1931.** https://repository.si.edu/bitstream/handle/10088/23920/SMC_85_Swanton_1931_8_1-46.pdf . Actual53-page PDF parsed by web; introduction printed1–4 and Abihka13–15 read, including explanation of changes from earlier information. Links Jim Star’s1912 information with1929 revisits, identifies Abihka/Talladega nearHenryetta. Counted as a distinct fieldwork study, not a duplicate of1922’s historical synthesis. The profile summarizes documentation history without reproducing sacred procedures, medicinal ingredients, layouts, or photographs.
9. **Charles Gibson, “Creek War Whoop,” Indian Journal, January31,1902; edited by Thomas Murray.** https://ualrexhibits.org/tribalwriters/artifacts/Gibson_Selected-works.html . Read author biography, actual essay, and editorial footnotes. This is a substantive Muscogee-authored primary text connecting Arbeka’s Gate Town role to Chitto Harjo and allotment-era politics. Gibson belongs to Tuckabatchee; he does not speak officially for Arbeka. His historical text and Murray’s footnotes are separate authorial layers. Collection contains satire and offensive historical descriptions elsewhere; no generic claim that everything in it is ethnographic fact.
10. **Kenneth W. McIntosh, OHS specialist biography, “Harjo, Chitto.”** https://www.okhistory.org/publications/enc/entry?entry=HA020 . Full entry read. Directly locates Harjo’s birth at Arbeka and connects his organizing to its protective role; follows anti-allotment activity and persecution. Some dates around his disappearance/death are approximate; profile does not overstate them. Individual political biography is not evidence of unanimous Arbeka political views. Further-reading book references do not count as separately read sources.
11. **Morgan Taylor, Mvskoke Media, December28,2022, Yardeka community center.** https://www.mvskokemedia.com/yardeka-community-center-holds-an-event-to-call-for-members/ . Full report read. Arbeka and Hickory Ground explicitly identified within the center’s expanded area. Concrete civic organizing, health/service fair, volunteer events and youth participation. Included for the community setting and interviews, not to equate a service center with a tribal town or ceremonial authority. Dated membership/meeting/contact details not given as present instructions.
12. **Morgan Taylor, Mvskoke Media, August8,2022, “Stompdance comes back after two years.”** https://www.mvskokemedia.com/stompdance-comes-back-after-two-years/ . Entire article read, especially Sten Joddi’s extended account of family belonging to Arbeka and cultural reconnection with daughters. More than a name in passing: a distinct first-person family experience relevant to continuity and participation. The story clearly calls the festival event a demonstration, includes different views of demonstration events, and does not document a private Arbeka ceremony. No claims that interviewees represent all Arbeka people or grounds.
